Where Does the Return Current Flow?

Top view of a trace routed over a solid ground plane. The heat map shows return-current density in the plane. At low frequency the return takes the path of least resistance (the direct line between the ground connections). At high frequency it takes the path of least inductance: directly under the trace. Cut a slot in the plane and watch what happens.

Why: the source must supply V = L·di/dt + I·R around the loop. When ωL ≫ R (above roughly 10–100 kHz for typical traces), the current redistributes to minimize L, which means minimizing loop area. The smallest loop is the one where the return runs directly beneath the signal, spread over roughly ±3h, with density ∝ 1/(1+(x/h)²).
The slot: the return current can't cross the gap, so it detours around the end of the slot. The loop area, inductance and radiated field all go up, and the slot itself radiates. This is why you never route a fast signal across a split plane. If a signal must cross a gap, put a stitching capacitor across the gap right next to the trace.
